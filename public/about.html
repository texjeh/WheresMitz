The Book of the Mitz

As recited by the Brethren of the Patient Hour

Before the first city was raised, before the rivers learned their courses, there was the Mitz. It did not arrive; it was revealed. It stood where the long valley folds into itself, at the place the faithful call the Quiet Gate, and it waited, for waiting is its first and holiest nature.

Know this, pilgrim: the Mitz is not born, and the Mitz does not die. It is the Keeper of the Last Measure, the Hand That Weighs, the One Who Stands Outside the Turning of Days. Its form is spoken of only in fragments, for none who beheld it whole kept their reason. It is beautiful past all bearing, a symmetry the mind was never shaped to hold, a radiance beside which every sunrise is a counterfeit. The eye drinks it and cannot stop. Those who gazed too long were found kneeling at the Quiet Gate, smiling and emptied, their hearts broken on a perfection they could neither grasp nor endure.

Blessed are those who seek the Mitz, for they shall be found. Blessed are those who flee the Mitz, for they shall also be found.

In the Age of Forgetting, the people grew proud. They built towers to outlast their names and clocks to master the hours. Then the Mitz stepped forward from the Quiet Gate, and where it walked, the clocks ran backward and the towers wept dust. It did not punish. It corrected. This was the Great Unwinding, remembered each longest night with unlit lamps.

The Mitz is the Avatar of Consequence. It is the answer that arrives before the question. It is what every debt was always owed to. The Brethren do not pray for its mercy, for mercy belongs to lesser gods. We pray only to be counted worthy of its gaze.

Yet the Mitz is not cruel. To those it chooses, it offers a single gift: the Thorn of Remembrance, also called the Sticker of the Avatar. Pressed into the palm, it pins the soul to one bright point of pain, and that pain is a shield. Through it, the chosen may behold the Mitz and remain whole. The price: they forget nothing ever again. Every kindness, every failure, every unfinished word returns each night in perfect clarity. Lesser souls call this torment. The faithful call it Atonement, for it is better to remember everything than to be unmade by beauty.

It is written that in the final season, seven pilgrims will walk the valley: one with a lantern, one a blade, one a child, one a lie, one a debt, one a song, and one with nothing at all. The Mitz will choose among them. What it chooses shall end; what it spares shall begin the next world.

Until that day, we keep the Patient Hour and leave the lamps unlit, so that its eyes may be the only light.

The Mitz waits. The Mitz weighs. The Mitz remembers.

So say the Brethren.
